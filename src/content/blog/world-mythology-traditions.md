Walk the mythology shelf in any general bookshop and count. Greek, Greek, Norse, Greek, Egyptian, Greek. There is nothing wrong with Greek mythology — it is superbly documented, endlessly retold, and it happens to be the tradition Western education carries. But it is one of a great many, and its dominance on the shelf is a fact about publishing rather than a fact about the world.

This is a starting point for the rest. Nineteen traditions, in the order they appear in *Codex Mythologica*, each with one story to begin on and a sentence about why that one.

## The four you have probably met

**Greek** — *Prometheus the Firebringer*. Start here rather than with Heracles: the labours are a list, and Prometheus is an argument. A god steals fire for people and is punished forever for it, and the story has never stopped being about something.

**Egyptian** — *The Murder of Osiris*. The founding crime of the tradition, and the reason for almost everything else in it. Isis reassembling her husband is where Egyptian myth's peculiar preoccupations — order, decay, restoration — all begin.

**Norse** — *The Binding of Fenrir*. Better than Ragnarok as an opening, because it is small. The gods must chain a wolf who is growing too fast, and the only way to do it is to lie to him, and one of them must put a hand in his mouth as surety. It tells you what kind of gods these are in four pages.

**Roman** — *Romulus and Remus*. Read it as a foundation story rather than as Greek myth with different names, and it is far stranger: a city founded by a fratricide, and the Romans told it about themselves on purpose.

## The five with deep literatures

**Hindu** — *The Churning of the Ocean of Milk*. Gods and demons pull a mountain back and forth on a serpent to make the sea give up its treasures. It is cosmology as an engineering problem, and it is very old.

**Japanese** — *Amaterasu in the Cave of Heaven*. The sun hides in a cave and the world goes dark; the other gods get her out with a party loud enough to make her curious. A rare myth in which the crisis is solved by mischief rather than force.

**Mesopotamian** — *Gilgamesh and the Quest for Immortality*. The oldest long story we have. A king refuses to accept that his friend is dead, and the answer he gets is not the one he went for.

**Celtic** — *The Children of Lir*. Four children turned into swans for nine hundred years. Irish myth is unusually willing to let sorrow simply last, and this is the clearest case.

**Persian** — the tradition where good and evil are genuinely symmetrical opponents rather than a strong god and a rebel, which changes the shape of every story in it.

## The ten the shelf usually skips

**Aztec** — *The Five Suns*. Four worlds have already been made and destroyed; ours is the fifth and it is also temporary. Almost no other tradition is this direct about the universe being on a clock.

**Mayan** — a separate literature from the Aztec one, with the Popol Vuh at its centre, and routinely confused with it by people who should know better.

**Korean** — founding myths with bears and tigers in them, and a strand of storytelling that runs straight into modern Korean film.

**Slavic** — reconstructed from later sources and folk survival rather than from a scripture, which makes it harder to write about honestly and more interesting when someone does.

**West & Central African** — the trickster traditions, among others; the ancestor of a great deal of storytelling in the Americas by a route nobody chose.

**Polynesian** — navigation, islands fished out of the sea, and Māui, who is the best trickster in world myth and is not widely read outside the Pacific.

**Inuit** — Sedna at the bottom of the sea, and a body of story shaped by an environment that forgives nothing.

**Turkic & Tengri** — the sky-god traditions of the steppe, poorly served in English and closer to the centre of Eurasian history than the shelf suggests.

**Arabian** — pre-Islamic and later material, of which most English readers know one frame story and none of the rest.

## How to actually read them

Two suggestions, both learned the hard way.

**Read a whole tradition before comparing traditions.** Comparative mythology is a real and useful field, and it is also the fastest way to flatten nineteen literatures into a diagram of archetypes. Read seven Norse stories in a row and Norse myth starts to have a temperament. Read one from each of nineteen and you get a taxonomy.

**Check who is retelling.** A myth reaches English through a chain — an oral teller, an ethnographer, a translator, a reteller — and every link chooses. A retelling that will not name its sources is not necessarily wrong, but it has decided you should not be able to check.

## The book

**[Codex Mythologica](/books/codex-mythologica)** collects **76 myths from these 19 traditions**, each retold at full length rather than summarised — the difference between a story and an entry in an encyclopaedia of stories. 329 pages in paperback, hardcover and a large-print edition.

For younger readers, **[The Great Book of World Myths](/books/the-great-book-of-world-myths)** does the same job for ages 8–12: 45 myths from 22 cultures, built deliberately against the eighty-per-cent-Greek shelf — no culture gets more than four stories and Greece gets no more than three. It has a hand-drawn world map, per-culture cards and a sourced pronunciation guide, and it says plainly which material it excludes and why.
