Phase 1 — Premium Cover Prompt Library

Five prompts, written and not run · public-domain factory · 6 September 2026

Nothing here has been generated. No image model was called.

This library is a set of instructions for the Founder to run later, at a time of his choosing, on an account of his own. It contains no generated image and no reference to one, and running it is a decision with three consequences that are set out below before the prompts, because they are the part that is easy to skip.

Before you run any of these

1. The type is not in the image, and must never be. Every Valice cover sets its own type — author, title, subtitle, series line, imprint — in BUILD/build_cover.py, from project_config.json. The image is the ground it is set on. Every prompt below therefore ends with an absolute prohibition on letters, and every prompt leaves the upper two-thirds of the frame quiet, because that is where the title block lands. An image that comes back with a beautiful invented word on it is a failed image, not a bonus.

2. Generating a cover changes the book's AI disclosure. All five Phase 1 books currently declare images: none, because their covers are drawn from vector primitives by script. A generated cover makes that answer images: generated, in project_config.json, in the KDP form at every upload and republish, and in the book's rights ledger row. The precedent to copy is RL-0070 in valice-house/rights/ledger.csv, written for the one generated cover this press has: it records the model, the account, the terms under which the output is owned, the cost, and the fact that the prompt forbade all writing.

3. There is a budget and it is enforced. scripts/covers/generate-cover.mjs reads the key only from OPENAI_API_KEY, is dry-run by default, refuses to spend past OPENAI_IMAGE_BUDGET_USD (default $4.00), and writes every call to assets/.image-ledger.json and the house cost ledger. Output lands in assets/<slug>/cover/generated/ and never in a cover slot: promoting an image to front-v1.png is a founder decision made by renaming after looking at it.

The command

Dry run first. It prints the request and calls nothing.

Two images per book at 1024×1536 is the sensible first pass: enough to choose between, cheap enough to run five books inside the cap. Upscale the chosen one to 2400×3600 before it goes into a slot.

What to check when an image comes back

CheckWhy
No letters anywhere, at any size, including on labels, spines and sealsThe model invents script that looks like language and is not. It reads as a forgery at any size above a thumbnail.
The upper two-thirds is quietThe title block is set over it. Test by opening the image beside the current cover at 150 px wide.
Nothing in it is a real, identifiable object from a museumA recognisable artefact brings a photograph's rights question with it, and this press does not publish an image it cannot clear.
No facesA face on a public-domain cover is read as a portrait of the author, and none of these five authors may be portrayed by a model.
The period is right to the decade, not to the centuryGould's drawer is Victorian; Seneca's clock is Roman. A model will happily put a Victorian magnifying glass on a Roman table.
It survives a 150 px thumbnailQA/cover.json already records a thumbnail contrast figure for the typographic covers; a premium cover must beat it, not merely differ from it.

1. Epictetus: The Discourses and Enchiridion

Valice Classics 1 · 176 pp · epictetus-discourses-and-enchiridion

What the book is. A freed slave's lectures on what is and is not in our power, taken down by a pupil in the second century and translated by George Long in 1877.

What the cover has to do. The book's whole argument is a division: what is up to you, and what is not. The cover should show a division, not a philosopher.

Why this image and not another. The collar is the one fact about Epictetus everybody remembers and the one he never mentions. Broken and set down, it is the book's subject exactly: the thing that was not in his power, and the thing that was.

2. Seneca: Selected Dialogues

Valice Classics 2 · 154 pp · seneca-selected-dialogues

What the book is. Five dialogues — anger, the shortness of life, the happy life, tranquillity, providence — in Aubrey Stewart's translation.

What the cover has to do. Seneca wrote to a friend about time, and died by the emperor's order. The cover should be about time, not about death.

Why this image and not another. The shortness of life is the best-known of the five dialogues, and a half-emptied clock says it without a skull or an hourglass, both of which would be a century wrong and a mood wrong.

Then, in a second pass if the first is close but busy: same image, less detail in the upper two thirds, nothing there but even light and plain ground.

3. Myths and Legends of China

Valice Classics 3 · 108 pp · myths-and-legends-of-china

What the book is. E. T. C. Werner's 1922 account of the Chinese divine order — volume one, the gods: the celestial ministries and how they are arranged.

What the cover has to do. This volume is about an ADMINISTRATION of heaven, not about dragons. The cover should show a bureaucracy that happens to be divine.

Why this image and not another. Werner's argument is that the Chinese pantheon is a civil service. Nine seals on nine cords, ranked, is that argument as an object — and it avoids every dragon-and-lantern cliché the subject attracts.

4. Indian Myth and Legend

Valice Classics 4 · 94 pp · indian-myth-and-legend

What the book is. Donald Mackenzie's 1913 account of the Vedic gods — volume one, the five chapters on the oldest layer.

What the cover has to do. The Vedic gods are weather and fire and the sacrifice. The cover should be elemental and pre-figurative: these gods had no images.

Why this image and not another. The Vedic religion is a fire and a formula, not a pantheon of portraits. An altar under an enormous sky is the honest picture, and it keeps the cover clear of the temple sculpture that belongs to a much later India.

The prompt

Then, in a second pass if the first is close but busy: same image, less detail in the upper two thirds, nothing there but even light and plain ground.

5. Mythical Monsters

Valice Classics 5 · 74 pp · mythical-monsters

What the book is. Charles Gould's 1886 attempt to argue that dragons were real animals — volume one, the three chapters on the dragon.

What the cover has to do. Gould is a Victorian naturalist arguing a wrong thing carefully. The cover should look like natural history, not like fantasy.

Why this image and not another. The book's interest is that a careful man used real bones to argue for a creature that never lived. A specimen drawer says that; a dragon on a cover would say the opposite and would sell the book to the wrong reader.

If none of them is good enough

The five covers that exist are typographic, drawn by script, cost nothing, carry no disclosure and are honest about what they are. They are not a placeholder. A generated cover is worth having only if it is better than the one it replaces at 150 pixels wide — which is the size at which almost every buying decision about these books will be made.

If two passes do not produce that, the right answer is to keep the typographic cover and spend the attention on the book.
