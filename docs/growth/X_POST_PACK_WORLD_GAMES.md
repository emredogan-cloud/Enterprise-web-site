## The thread (post first, pin it)

**1/**
The boards came out of the royal graves at Ur in the 1920s.

Nobody could play them for another sixty years.

What unlocked the game was not an excavation. It was a Babylonian scribe's homework, written in cuneiform, read by a curator at the British Museum. 🧵
*(image: ur-board.png)*

**2/**
The board's odd shape — a block of twelve, a bridge of two, a block of six — turns out to be the whole design.

Each player has a private start and a private exit. Only the middle is shared.

Everything dangerous happens in the middle.

**3/**
Four dice, two marked corners each. Count the marked corners: 0 means you lose the turn, 4 means you move four and throw again.

Four coins do exactly the same job. Count heads.

**4/**
Land on a rosette: throw again.

The rosette in the middle of the shared lane cannot be attacked. A piece parked there is safe, profitable, and sits on the one square everything else has to pass.

Take it early and the middle lane becomes a toll gate.

**5/**
Honest line: the board is archaeology. The rules are a scholar's reading of one terse tablet. The tablet also records betting and star-signs for the pieces — those are not rules and we don't print them as rules.

## Single posts (one a day)

**A — Kalah**
The mancala board in most toy shops is Kalah.

It is an American commercial game from the 1940s.

The family it borrows from — Oware, Bao, Omweso, Pallanguzhi — is far older and far more interesting. Which one to learn first, and why: valicepress.com/blog/mancala-which-game-to-start-with
*(image: oware-capture.png)*

**B — Chinese checkers**
Chinese checkers is German. Published in 1892 as Stern-Halma, a star-shaped variant of Halma, which is American, from the 1880s.

The "Chinese" was added later in the US because it was thought to sell games.

Seven of these, with sources: valicepress.com/blog/board-game-origin-myths

**C — Senet**
Senet was played in Egypt for three thousand years. Boards were buried with kings. Nefertari is painted playing it alone on her tomb wall.

Then the rules vanished. What survives is the board and five marked squares.

Here is one honest way to play it anyway: valicepress.com/blog/senet-rules-reconstruction
*(image: senet-board.png)*

**D — Oware's two rules**
Two habits of the Akan game Oware are written into the rules, not left to manners:

You may never starve your opponent.
You may never take everything.

If a capture would empty their side, the seeds stay where they are.

**E — Hnefatafl**
For a game the sagas mention constantly, almost nothing about hnefatafl was written down.

The only tafl game with a documented rule set is Sámi tablut — because Carl Linnaeus watched it played in Lapland in 1732 and wrote it down.

Every "Viking chess" rulebook is rebuilt from that.
*(image: hnefatafl-setup.png)*

**F — Patolli**
Patolli is the one game in the book that was banned.

The Spanish outlawed it within a generation of the conquest and burned the mats — because it was played for stakes, and because players called on a god before throwing the beans.
*(image: patolli-cross.png)*

**G — The Kurna board**
"The Temple of Kurna carries a morris board cut in 1400 BC."

The board exists. The date does not. A grid scratched into a stone floor cannot be dated by the floor.

We print the board. We don't print the date.
*(image: morris-board.png)*

**H — Sorted by mechanism**
Most collections file games by country.

We filed 56 games by how they work: sowing · hunt and siege · race · line and territory · the war board · chance and nerve · no board at all.

Put that way, the ancestors of chess sit together and you watch six cultures solve the same problem six ways.

**I — What to use instead**
Almost nothing in a traditional-games book has to be bought.

An egg carton is a mancala board. Four coins are four Sumerian dice. Buttons are pieces. Split lolly sticks are Egyptian throwing sticks.

The book says, for every game, what to use instead of what.
